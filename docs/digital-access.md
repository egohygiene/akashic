# Get connected and protect essential accounts

Start with the task you need to complete: read a notice, apply for help, join a class, or regain your own account. This U.S.-focused first edition connects existing [Security](../lists/security/README.md#digital-access-and-account-safety), library, connectivity, disability, and consumer-help resources. Public sources were checked September 18, 2026; costs, eligibility, recovery options, and device support can change.

## 1. Find a connection, device, or person to help

| Basic skills or patient help | [DigitalLearn](../lists/awesome-abundance/libraries-and-local-access/README.md#learning-and-research-platforms) and your library's digital-skills staff | Free browser lessons cover computers, email, accounts, forms, and video calls; login is optional. | Community technical help | Human-I-T's current [support terms](https://www.human-i-t.org/tech-support-overview/) | Free support has device-recipient, purchaser, or partner-service conditions; confirm eligibility and duration. It is not a provider's account-recovery authority. |

Before buying a device, check that its supported operating system, browser, camera, storage, and assistive features can perform your actual task. Bring a description of the barrier to a helper; enter passwords and codes yourself. If only a shared computer is available, ask about its privacy and deletion policy, avoid saving credentials, sign out of each account, and remove downloaded private files. Private browsing does not make a shared or monitored device trustworthy.

**Need a person without completing an online form?** Call or visit your public library using its official listing and ask who provides digital navigation. If access or communication is the barrier, the disability guide has provider and non-web contact routes. [Atlas](../atlas/README.md) supplies existing place-specific starting points; confirm current service area and availability with the provider.

## 2. Set up access you can maintain

Use the provider's official app or website, and confirm whether the account is personal, employer/school-managed, or tied to a specific benefits service. Check age, country, fees, phone-number, and device requirements. Keep recovery contact details you control current; avoid a recovery plan that depends entirely on the same locked account or lost phone.

[CISA Secure Our World](../lists/security/README.md#account-security-and-incident-readiness) explains unique strong passwords, password managers, multifactor authentication (MFA), software updates, and phishing. Choose a manager that works with your devices and access needs; understand its recovery and export process. Existing [password and authenticator tools](../lists/awesome-abundance/free-and-open-source-software/README.md#privacy-and-security) stay in their software collection. Some options need an account, paid features, or local database backups.

Enable a supported second factor and privately retain the provider's backup/recovery method before replacing a phone or deleting an authenticator. Check how to recover both the account and the password manager. Never approve an unexpected sign-in request or give a caller, helper, or “recovery agent” your current password, one-time code, or recovery key. Provider forms may request specific historical evidence; enter it only in the verified official flow.

## 3. Back up files and choose privacy settings

The [NCSC backup guide](../lists/security/README.md#account-security-and-incident-readiness) covers separate copies, cloud accounts, removable storage, and restoration. Back up important documents and photos, protect the copy, and test restoring a non-sensitive file. Check what the service includes: synchronization can propagate deletion, and a backup that requires the inaccessible account may not help during lockout. Disconnect removable backup media when finished. Cloud storage can have quotas, paid upgrades, and network costs; external storage also costs money. Keep recovery information securely accessible independently of the device it unlocks.

Use the [FTC privacy guide](../lists/security/README.md#privacy-and-personal-data) to review browser tracking, advertising, account visibility, and app access to location, photos, contacts, camera, and microphone. Grant only what the task needs. Private browsing does not stop all tracking; privacy settings do not erase information already shared. If someone may be monitoring or controlling your device, use the [survivor-centered safety routes](../lists/security/README.md#creator-public-facing-and-harassment-safety) before changes that could alert them.

## 4. Pause before an unexpected message or offer

The [FTC phishing guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams) explains fake account warnings, invoices, refunds, and requests for information. Reach the organization through a known official app, statement, or independently verified website instead of the message's link or phone number. A familiar logo, urgent deadline, or polished wording is not verification. Be wary of unsolicited remote-access requests or anyone charging to “unlock” an account.

If you disclosed information or money, promptly contact the affected institution through its verified route. Use the [consumer-help and fraud routes](../lists/personal-finance-and-insurance/README.md#transfers-and-consumer-help) from #136 for complaints and identity-theft recovery. Those processes are separate from restoring an online login and do not guarantee reimbursement.

## 5. Lost access to an essential account

You do not need to complete the earlier sections first. Use a device you have reason to trust; if monitoring or abuse is involved, get safety-planning help first.

1. **Start at the official provider.** Open its sign-in/help page directly and select the path for a forgotten password, missing factor, compromised account, or disabled account. These are different problems. For managed work/school accounts, contact the actual administrator. A social-media “recovery expert” is not that authority.
2. **Protect the service deadline.** If a health, benefits, banking, school, or utility task cannot wait, ask that institution for an accessible alternate channel and confirmation of your contact. A recovery request does not submit an application, pay a bill, or extend a deadline. Avoid making duplicate service applications without checking.
3. **Follow the provider's evidence and waiting instructions.** Keep the case reference, date, notices, and next step privately. Preserve relevant messages/screenshots without posting account details, documents, tokens, or codes in public forums. Do not wipe a device or discard an existing session before checking the provider's instructions; individual recovery routes differ.
4. **After control returns, check what changed.** Replace compromised/reused passwords, review recovery contacts, active sessions, connected apps, email forwarding, and unauthorized activity; enable MFA with a backup method. Use the [FTC recovery checklist](../lists/security/README.md#account-security-and-incident-readiness). If fraudulent messages were sent, warn affected contacts through a safe channel.
5. **Confirm the original task is complete.** Verify the application, payment, notice, or appointment separately and record a follow-up date. If recovery fails, ask the institution about its remaining official options. Creating a new email account does not transfer benefits, purchases, data, or ownership of the old account.

### Provider examples and limits

These public help pages are free to read. Recovery needs provider-specific proof and may fail; no page below is an account-access bypass or a guarantee of human support.

| Account | Official instructions | Material limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google / Gmail | [Recover your Google Account](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7682439) | Work/school accounts may need an administrator. Google warns against third-party recovery services and does not offer telephone sign-in recovery. |
| Microsoft personal account | [Help with the recovery form](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accounts-billing/manage/help-with-the-microsoft-account-recovery-form) | Try the linked Sign-in Helper first. The form cannot overcome two-step verification when all alternate verification methods are inaccessible; support cannot bypass this. |
| Apple Account | [Account recovery when you cannot reset the password](https://support.apple.com/en-us/118574) | Try the listed trusted-device/contact options first. Recovery may take days or longer; support cannot shorten the wait. Follow the specific device-use instructions, since account use can cancel recovery. |
| Another provider or public-service portal | Its official sign-in help, or provider links in the FTC recovery checklist | Confirm who owns the login and who delivers the service. Technical support, eligibility decisions, complaints, and appeals are separate routes. |

## 6. Keep a small private follow-through record

Record only what helps you act: **service/task · official route · date contacted · case reference · response or waiting instruction · alternate service route · next action/deadline**. Keep required evidence separately and securely. Do not put passwords, recovery codes, identity documents, or completed examples in this repository.

For identity theft, use the existing [IdentityTheft.gov route](../lists/awesome-abundance/public-programs-and-services/README.md#taxes-credit-and-consumer-help). For official identity documents, use the [U.S. identity and civic-paperwork chapter](civic/us-identity.md) and confirm the issuing agency's official route for the document you need. For disability access, return to [#144's guide](../lists/awesome-abundance/README.md#disability-access-and-independent-living). For another person's account after death, use [authorized digital-legacy requests](../lists/family-caregiving-and-aging/README.md#digital-legacy-and-account-requests).
